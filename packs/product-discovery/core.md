# Product discovery: Core pack

Use this pack before building something whose value is unproven — a new product, a feature nobody asked for twice, or anything justified by "users will want this."

## Does this problem exist outside this room?

**Default answer pattern:** Find evidence of the problem in behavior that already happened — a workaround someone built, a spreadsheet they maintain, money they already spend, a support ticket they filed. Absence of a workaround usually means absence of a problem worth paying for.

**Tradeoffs:** Evidence-gathering delays building and can kill an idea the team is attached to. It is far cheaper than the same discovery after launch.

**Anti-patterns:** Validating with people who like you. Counting enthusiasm as demand. Treating a competitor's existence as proof of a market without asking whether anyone actually uses them.

**Escalate when:** No evidence exists and the team wants to build anyway. That is a legitimate bet, but it should be named as one and sized accordingly.

**Verify:** Name one person who currently spends time or money on this problem, and what specifically they do today.

## Are you asking about the past or the future?

**Default answer pattern:** Ask what they did, not what they would do. "Walk me through the last time this happened" produces data. "Would you use this?" produces politeness.

**Tradeoffs:** Past-focused questions feel indirect and take longer to get to the point. They are the only kind whose answers survive contact with a launch.

**Anti-patterns:** Pitching the idea and then asking for reactions. Any question containing "would you". Compliments recorded as validation.

**Escalate when:** The behavior being asked about is genuinely new and has no past. Then only a cheap real-stakes test will tell you anything.

**Verify:** Review the notes. Any answer describing the future rather than the past is opinion, not evidence.

## Is this a problem or a preference?

**Default answer pattern:** Look for cost already being paid — hours, money, risk, or embarrassment. A problem people pay to avoid is real; one they merely dislike is a preference and will not convert.

**Tradeoffs:** This filter discards many pleasant ideas, including ones that would genuinely improve a user's day.

**Anti-patterns:** Ranking features by how often they were mentioned. Confusing a loud minority with an urgent need. Building for the problem your best-liked customer described.

**Escalate when:** Something is strategically important but not yet painful — a compliance deadline, a platform deprecation. Urgency is external, and the decision is the user's.

**Verify:** Name what the problem currently costs, in a unit that can be counted.

## What is the cheapest way to find out this is wrong?

**Default answer pattern:** Identify the single assumption that would sink the idea, and test that one first with the smallest artifact that can produce a real signal — a landing page, a manual concierge run, a prototype, a spreadsheet operated by hand.

**Tradeoffs:** Cheap tests give noisier answers and can mislead when the fake experience differs from the real one.

**Anti-patterns:** Building the whole thing to test it. Testing the assumption the team is most confident about. A "pilot" that costs a quarter.

**Escalate when:** The riskiest assumption cannot be tested without real money or real users. That threshold is the user's to authorize.

**Verify:** State the result that would stop the project. If no result would, the test is theatre.

## Who is this for, specifically?

**Default answer pattern:** Name a narrow group who share the problem, reachable through a channel you can actually name. Broad definitions cannot be validated or marketed.

**Tradeoffs:** Narrow targeting feels like leaving the market on the table, and sometimes does. It is what makes the first version good enough for anyone to switch.

**Anti-patterns:** "Developers." "Small businesses." A persona invented rather than observed. Defining the audience by the feature rather than the problem.

**Escalate when:** The narrow group is too small to sustain the effort. That is a business viability question.

**Verify:** Describe how to reach fifty of them this week. Vagueness in the answer means vagueness in the definition.

## What would make you stop?

**Default answer pattern:** Write the kill criteria before starting, with a number and a date. Ideas without a stopping condition do not get stopped; they get quietly maintained forever.

**Tradeoffs:** Explicit criteria can end something prematurely, before an idea that needed longer had time.

**Anti-patterns:** Success metrics with no failure threshold. Moving the goalposts after seeing results. "Let's give it another quarter" repeated indefinitely.

**Escalate when:** Kill criteria have been met and the team wants to continue. That is a legitimate call, but it should be made deliberately and recorded.

**Verify:** Show the criteria to someone uninvolved and ask whether they could tell, from the numbers alone, that it was time to stop.
